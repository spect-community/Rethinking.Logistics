## Challenge

Most European warehouses are built speculatively: at the design stage nobody knows who the tenant will be, what they will store or handle, or how long they will stay. Developers manage this uncertainty through standardisation and a simple rule — bigger is safer. Floor area, clear height, floor loads, docks, parking and building services are sized for any possible tenant rather than for a known one.

The result is a building that fits many tenants adequately and none precisely. Oversizing costs embodied carbon, land and money, and the parts that a future tenant would value most (daylight, staff facilities, connection to the neighbourhood) are the first to be cut, because they cannot be priced at the sale.

## Why it matters

- The first owner is usually a developer-trader who sells at or before completion, so long-term operating benefits are not captured in the developer's return.
- Projects where the user is known early, such as [[Bestseller Logistics Center West]] and its predecessor in Haderslev, show that a precise brief allows a precise and higher-quality design.
- Industry partners report building services oversized by up to 50 % because requirements are poorly formulated (partner estimate).

## Possible levers (open question)

- **Process:** know the tenant earlier (build-to-suit instead of speculative).
- **Design:** adaptability and modularity instead of oversizing.
- **Market:** long-hold investment models (see [[Nexus Logistics Park]]).
- **City:** quality requirements as a condition of the permit.

## Common misreading

**Not this:** every capacity sized for the largest possible tenant "to be safe".
**Why it fails:** most tenants never use the extra capacity, while its carbon, land and cost are paid in full.
**Do this:** design a base that fits the likely range of tenants and can be extended or adapted.
**Measure with:** [[Oversizing ratio]], [[Space utilisation]], [[Adaptability]].
**Evidence:** expert statements; no measured case yet.
