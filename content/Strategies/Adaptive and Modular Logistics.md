## Principle

A logistics building is designed for change rather than for a single tenant at a single moment. Its structure, envelope and site are planned so that it can shrink and grow with demand, be reconfigured, moved, taken apart and given a second life — long life, loose fit.

## Why it matters

Logistics changes faster than buildings: tenants, automation and delivery models turn over within years, and a hall that fits only one of them is demolished early ([[Adaptable and relocatable logistics structures]]). Logistics tenants typically sign leases of 5–7 years, while the building is expected to last far longer ([[Lease length is the hidden design brief]]). Inside those leases, occupiers deliberately keep slack — perhaps 50–70% utilisation for most of the year — so that they can reach full capacity before Christmas, which means buildings are sized and heated for an annual peak ([[Grey space – buildings sized for Black Friday]]).

The market is already sorting stock that can adapt from stock that cannot: in the Netherlands over 80% of vacant logistics space is second-hand, and retrofitting older sheds is often impractical ([[Old sheds are becoming unlettable]]). Every early demolition wastes the carbon already spent in the structure ([[Embodied carbon and adaptive reuse of logistics buildings]]).

## Projects

Four student projects test the idea at four scales — a room that breathes, a building that travels, a structure that spans infrastructure and a wall made of reused containers.

- [[Elsa Hauksdottir]] reworks a steel warehouse of about 11,000 m² in Reykjavík, built 14 m from apartment blocks, into an "elastic" building: movable partitions and foldable racks shrink the heated storage zone off-peak and free the rest for markets and exhibitions.
- [[Amy Naprstek]] proposes a nomadic storage system of prefabricated, relocatable blocks in three sizes that combine storage with a temporary public programme; each configuration stays from 3–5 months up to 3 years, after which the plot returns to meadow.
- [[Julia Kopác]] lifts modular warehouse, retail and crossing modules over highway cuttings on the edge of Prague, so the building acts as a noise barrier against traffic noise of up to about 75 dB and the ground is freed for greenery and footpaths.
- [[Sara Sulollari]] adapts a port logistics building in Reykjavík with a courtyard extension and a U-shaped wind buffer built from 85 reused shipping containers that opens the waterfront to the public.

## Trade-offs

- **Speed against cost.** Robotic partitions reconfigure in minutes and need less labour, but cost more to operate and have a higher environmental impact than manual ones, so the slower manual system was chosen ([[Elsa Hauksdottir]]).
- **Light and movable against warm.** Lightweight relocatable blocks have low thermal insulation, which limits climate-sensitive goods; the truck emissions of moving them are not yet counted ([[Amy Naprstek]]).
- **Open against closed.** A façade open for daylight and public access conflicts with the compact thermal envelope logistics needs, so the façade switches between the two modes ([[Elsa Hauksdottir]]).
- **Span against structure.** Building over infrastructure roughly doubles the cost to build in exchange for greenery, walkability and noise reduction ([[Julia Kopác]]); experts add that every extra metre of span means bigger beams, so flexibility has to be balanced against material ([[The biggest possible hall reflex is a carbon cost]]).
- **Reuse against performance.** Keeping an existing building saves upfront carbon, but if it cannot reach modern operational performance, whole-life carbon can end up higher ([[Reuse can lose on whole-life carbon]]); the reused port building has no energy study yet ([[Sara Sulollari]]).
- **Tailored against generic.** A building tailored to one heavily automated tenant may not be usable afterwards; developers aim instead at what most of the market needs ([[Tailoring is a one-way door]], [[80% of the market need, not 100% of one tenant]]).

## Evidence gaps

- [[Adaptability]] is the most used metric of this strategy, yet it is estimated by hand in every project; [[Design for disassembly score]] is a qualitative rating without a stated method ([[Evidence gaps]]).
- The embodied carbon and cost values for the bridging structure have no stated calculation, and tools that could compute [[Embodied carbon intensity]] and [[Whole-life carbon]] were not yet used in any project ([[Evidence gaps]]).
- No project quantifies the carbon saved by avoiding demolition or by relocation ([[Embodied carbon saved by reuse]]), nor the transport emissions of moving modules ([[Amy Naprstek]]).
- The heating reduction rests on a benchmark intensity whose source is not cited, and the visual-impact and well-being percentages have no method ([[Elsa Hauksdottir]]).
- Wind behaviour was simulated, but the student notes it cannot be validated without a wind engineer ([[Sara Sulollari]]).

## Regulatory observations

- Current life-cycle standards do not allow designers to model the carbon benefit of adaptability and disassembly, partly to prevent unverifiable claims of very long service life ([[Adaptability saves carbon but can't be counted]]). Where current standards limit the approach, its main benefit — avoided demolition — stays invisible in the assessment.
- Without a material passport, each reclaimed element has to be certified individually before structural use, and insurers are cautious ([[Certification of reused structural elements is the real blocker]]); this applies to reused containers, relocatable blocks and second-hand halls alike.
- Permitting is built around permanent buildings: expanding usually means a new planning process of two to three years ([[Expandable units are wanted but planning kills them]]), and the nomadic project caps each configuration at three years so that it can work as a temporary use ([[Amy Naprstek]]).
- Iceland has no rules on pedestrian-level wind comfort, so the wind buffer was justified by the student's own simulation rather than by a standard ([[Sara Sulollari]]).

## Questions for practice

- **Developers:** Could the grey space held for seasonal peaks be leased to public or civic uses off-peak, and how would that be written into the lease?
- **Designers:** Which spans, clear heights and joints give enough flexibility without paying for flexibility no one will use — and how is that recorded so the building can later be taken apart?
- **Municipalities:** Could a permit category for relocatable, time-limited logistics structures exist, with an obligation to return the land to its previous state?
- **Everyone:** How could avoided demolition be credited in carbon assessment, so that a building designed for a second life is rewarded at the point of decision?
