## Principle

A logistics building does not have to be a single-use box behind a fence: it can share its site, its section and its roof with public space, shops, production and community life. The trucks keep their own side, level or hours, while the rest of the building becomes part of the city — very often by reusing an industrial structure that already stands instead of demolishing it.

## Why it matters

Logistics tends to form rings of sprawl around cities rather than sitting within them ([[Logistics lives in the sprawl, not the city]]), and where the city grows up to it, the warehouse becomes a barrier: sealed yards, truck roads and blank façades next to housing ([[Urban integration of logistics buildings]], [[Visual impact and acceptance by neighbours]]). Sites stay closed to the public ([[Public access and community use of logistics sites]]), and where people and trucks do meet, safety becomes the first concern ([[Pedestrian–truck safety on logistics sites]]). At the same time, e-commerce pulls distribution closer to consumers, while empty post-industrial buildings in the city are demolished and their embodied carbon is lost ([[Embodied carbon and adaptive reuse of logistics buildings]], [[Last-mile delivery emissions and traffic]]).

The studio's answer is to treat function-mixing, not location, as the design lever — as one expert put it, a facility that must sit outside the city should carry additional functions ([[Single-function land use is the real waste]]).

## Projects

Four student projects work with this approach, from a single reused hall to a whole industrial zone.

- [[Sara Gani]] moves a warehouse next to housing in Pivoňská, Prague 14, partly buries it under a green roof that ramps down into a public park, community space, café/shop and a fox corridor, and puts trucks and docks on the far side; pedestrian-accessible area grows from 450 m² to 2,400 m², as reported by the student.
- [[Thomas Olsen]] converts an abandoned concrete-frame industrial building in Prague 9 into a last-mile hub: trucks and electric vans on one side, a landscaped public plaza on the other, storage on the upper floors and a biosolar roof.
- [[Vladi Kostecki]] keeps a former industrial plant on a Prague 9 brownfield and zones it into logistics, retained manufacturing and a public commercial wing, turns the adjoining brownfield into a 34,000 m² public park and separates residents from trucks with an underpass, as reported by the student.
- [[Ilinca Maria Baciu]] keeps the warehouses of the 876,000 m² Hostivice industrial zone working and lays a network of raised green platforms over and between them, carrying public routes, green roofs, photovoltaics and rainwater retention.

## Trade-offs

- **Public space against storage.** A campus layout gave residents more public space but lowered storage and was inefficient for trucks ([[Sara Gani]]); more green space on the property means less storage ([[Vladi Kostecki]]).
- **Openness against operations.** Opening the site raises social value but may add maintenance and conflicts with logistics ([[Thomas Olsen]]); public and green layers lower logistics efficiency and raise construction cost ([[Ilinca Maria Baciu]]).
- **Daylight against racking.** More windows improve workers' daylight but take wall space from shelving ([[Sara Gani]]).
- **Upfront against running carbon.** An upgraded envelope adds cost and embodied carbon to the reuse, but lowers operational energy ([[Vladi Kostecki]]).
- **Housing next door.** Experts consider housing and 24/7 logistics hard to combine in one block, while offices and retail coexist well ([[Residential and logistics cannot share a block; offices and retail can]]); how much truck noise still reaches the neighbours remains open ([[Sara Gani]]).

## Evidence gaps

- The carbon comparisons rest on floor area multiplied by benchmark values; the floor area and fleet inputs behind some figures are not shown ([[Thomas Olsen]], [[Vladi Kostecki]]).
- Several public-space, biodiversity, daylight and water values are given without a stated method ([[Sara Gani]]); in the Hostivice project most KPIs are listed but not yet calculated ([[Ilinca Maria Baciu]]).
- The mix of uses itself is not measured: [[Mixed-use ratio]] is named but not quantified, and [[Mixed-use ratio]], [[Pedestrian–vehicle conflict points]] and [[Last-mile delivery emissions]] are used without any calculation tool ([[Evidence gaps]]).
- No project calculates the noise reaching nearby housing, the variable that decides how close logistics and other uses can come.

## Regulatory observations

- Consumer access is generally not permitted on industrial plots, so retail and logistics meet only in a separate "retail warehousing" subsector at highway junctions ([[Retail warehousing exists because master plans forbid mixing people and trucks]]). Where current zoning keeps people and trucks on separate plots, it limits the hybrid approach; design-based separation — by level, side or time — could offer an alternative route.
- Above three storeys, fire requirements call for heavy steel or concrete, and rentable efficiency drops to about 75% once ramps and lifts are deducted ([[Stacking is normal — everywhere but here]]); this is one reason stacked logistics remains rare in Europe. In Czechia, master plans and municipalities rarely approve high-bay buildings of 20–30 m, while Poland has permitted 30 m ([[Czech height limits are quietly repelling investment]]).
- Residential use is not permitted inside a logistics master plan, which blocked temporary staff housing next to a manual distribution centre with a reported ~6,000 staff ([[350 people vs 6,000 next door]]); where current zoning separates work and housing this strictly, it limits the hybrid approach.
- Czech municipalities capture little tax or development contribution from industrial buildings, so they have little incentive to approve them ([[Mayors get trucks, not taxes]]); public amenities delivered with the building could become part of that conversation.

## Questions for practice

- **Developers:** Which uses — retail, workshops, sport, community — could share a logistics site on your terms, and on which side, level or hours would they sit?
- **Designers:** Before demolishing an industrial building, could a simple embodied-carbon comparison of reuse and new build be made at concept stage, as in the student projects?
- **Municipalities:** Could zoning allow public access on industrial plots where safety is proven by design, for example through vertical separation of people and trucks?
- **Everyone:** How close can housing come to a working logistics building, and which noise measurement should decide it?
