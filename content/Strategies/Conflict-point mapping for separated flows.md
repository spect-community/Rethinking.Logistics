## The question it answers

Where do people and trucks cross, and which layout removes those crossings?

## How it works

1. Draw the routes of trucks, vans, staff, cars and the public on the existing site plan.
2. Count the points where pedestrian routes cross vehicle routes, or the share of separated routes.
3. Redesign the zoning, entrances or crossings (for example an underpass) and count again.
4. Keep the layout with the fewest crossings.

## In the student projects

- [[Natalie Žižková]] — *manual / formula*. [[Pedestrian–vehicle conflict points]]: existing 4 → proposed 0. Decision: “The crossing-point and flow analysis led to a strict two-zone layout (truck area vs public/staff area) with zero pedestrian–truck crossings.”
- [[Vladi Kostecki]] — *manual / formula*. [[Pedestrian–vehicle conflict points]]: original 3; new 0. Decision: “…add a pedestrian underpass to remove all pedestrian–truck crossings.”
- [[Sara Gani]] — *manual / formula*. [[Pedestrian–vehicle conflict points]]: share of separated routes before 30%; after 90%. Decision: “The L-shaped layout … was kept because it scored best across the KPI matrix while separating truck, car and pedestrian flows.”
- [[Eva Kažukolovaitė]] — *manual / formula*. [[Pedestrian–vehicle conflict points]]: not quantified; the proposal claims conflicts with pedestrians are eliminated. Decision: “Replace the temporary container depot with rearrangeable timber modules placed in a cross-dock pattern to separate van and cargo-bike flows, move the vehicle entrance to remove pedestrian collisions.”

Values and decisions as reported by the student; see each case study for the page in the portfolio.

## Where it falls short

- The projects count crossings in two ways (number of points, or share of separated routes), so the results are not comparable.
- [[Worker safety incidents]] would need operational data; the projects assess safety only through the layout.
