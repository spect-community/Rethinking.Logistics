## Principle

A data centre is the newest kind of logistics building: it stores data instead of goods and turns almost all of its electricity into heat. Instead of exiling it to cheap land and cooling that heat away, the strategy plugs it into its surroundings — waste heat goes to heat networks, greenhouses and neighbours, the roof produces power for the site and the grid, and the location is chosen where that exchange is possible.

## Why it matters

Data centres are growing faster than the grids and cities around them. In the comparison used in the studio, an average conventional data centre consumes as much energy as roughly 20,000 households and a hyperscale one about 100,000 ([[From 20,000 households to a country]]). Cooling takes energy and, where traditional methods persist, large amounts of water ([[Waste heat and cooling of data centres]], [[Nobody agrees how much water they use]]). In Czechia, power is already the limiting factor for where such facilities can go ([[Hyperscale data centres skip Czechia for lack of power]]), and the energy a building consumes and produces on site is a design question in its own right ([[Operational energy and on-site renewables]]).

Yet most data centres already sit in or near cities ([[Most of them are already urban]]), beside homes that need heat and neighbours who see blank boxes ([[Visual impact and acceptance by neighbours]], [[Urban integration of logistics buildings]]). The studio asked whether this proximity can become an exchange rather than a conflict.

## Projects

Three student projects treat the data centre as part of a local energy system, at three very different scales.

- [[Katrin Rybinskiy]] keeps two 1950s factory halls in Kladno's former steelworks area as data centres, adds a third one with a fully PV-covered roof and a greenhouse, and routes the remaining waste heat through the nearby power plant to housing; paving gives way to ponds, trees and green corridors.
- [[Julia Godard Lombard]] inserts a storage data centre into the listed, long-neglected Desfourský Palace near Masarykovo station in Prague and pairs it with heat-hungry programmes — sauna and wellness, dry-food production, a café — in three phases with a growing data-centre share of 20%, 50% and 80%, exporting surplus heat to neighbours in later phases.
- [[Robin Jesenský]] designs HALECS, a row of modular server halls on the windy coast near Grindavík, Iceland, whose copper shells and cooling fins channel ground wind through the servers, with a closed water loop that could supply heat for residential hot water. The group sheet below also shows two projects of other strategies.

The exhibition also showed *Cloud in the City – Understanding Data Centers*, an explanatory poster without a student author. It presents the counter-scenario this strategy responds to: data centres as energy-, water- and land-hungry neighbours that strain the grid, pump water, create heat islands and noise, and sit next to housing while offering few local jobs — what happens when they are not integrated.

## Trade-offs

- **Roof for power against roof for nature.** A fully PV-covered roof raises renewable generation but leaves no room for a green roof, so green and blue area must come from the ground ([[Katrin Rybinskiy]]); maximising PV also adds structural load ([[Julia Godard Lombard]]).
- **More servers against more neighbours.** A higher data-centre share raises recoverable heat and the heat-recovery radius, but also energy and water use, and it pushes out the sauna and dry-food production that created most of the jobs ([[Julia Godard Lombard]]).
- **Reuse against efficiency.** Both urban projects reuse existing buildings to save carbon; experts caution that a less efficient data centre likely loses over its life, and that its embodied carbon sits mostly in the building services, not the shell ([[Don't force the wrong second life on a building]], [[In a data centre the embodied carbon is in the pipes]]).
- **Heat recovery does not remove cooling.** Data centres must run through the hottest summer day, so full chiller capacity is needed regardless of how much heat is reused ([[Data centres are giant heaters]]).
- **Wind against access.** The windiest sites for passive cooling were protected or hard to reach for heavy construction vehicles ([[Robin Jesenský]]).

## Evidence gaps

- Calculation methods for the PV yield and total heat are not shown, and income from reused heat and surplus energy was not assessed ([[Katrin Rybinskiy]]).
- How much renewable energy can be produced on site was an open question for one project, and carbon, land use and biodiversity appear only as relative bars ([[Julia Godard Lombard]]).
- Cooling water, energy efficiency and the passive cooling itself were not quantified or simulated ([[Robin Jesenský]]).
- [[Heat recovery radius]], [[Power usage effectiveness]] and [[Water consumption]] were used without a calculation tool, although tools that could compute [[Waste heat recovery]] were available ([[Evidence gaps]]).
- No project models how the heat would be delivered to a real district heating network — temperature, heat pump and pipe length — which experts name as the decisive factor ([[Brewing waste heat heats the public building]]).

## Regulatory observations

- In parts of northern Europe, heat recovery into district heating is a routine design and planning consideration whenever a data centre is submitted for approval ([[Waste heat is already an approval condition]]); where current standards do not ask for it, the exchange depends on the goodwill of the developer.
- Sharing energy between buildings in a city is already allowed under Czech energy law; a Panattoni warehouse in Rotterdam shows the model, carrying far more PV than it needs and sharing energy with local hospitals ([[A warehouse heating a hospital]]).
- Dublin steered data centres into chosen parts of the city as part of its energy and land-use planning; the lecturer described urban planning as a tool that is badly underused in Europe ([[Dublin planned for data centre demand instead of reacting to it]]).
- Data centres count as an industrial use but are not yet tracked in the sector's shared research data ([[Hyperscale data centres skip Czechia for lack of power]]), which makes planning for them harder.

## Questions for practice

- **Developers:** Could a site be chosen for its distance to the heat network and its grid connection first, and for the price of land second?
- **Designers:** Which neighbours — homes, greenhouses, pools, public buildings — can take the heat at the temperature the data centre actually delivers, and how far can it travel?
- **Municipalities:** Could heat recovery and a heat-sales agreement with the district heating company be part of the planning conditions, as in northern Europe?
- **Everyone:** Who takes responsibility for the equipment and the heat supply when the servers become obsolete after a few years?
