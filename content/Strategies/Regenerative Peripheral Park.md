## Principle

A logistics site on the urban edge is treated as landscape infrastructure rather than as a fenced box on asphalt. The building stays, the trucks stay — but the land around and on top of it is designed to restore habitat, ecological connectivity and the water cycle, and part of it is given back to the people who live next door.

## Why it matters

Warehouses are among the most land-hungry building types, and most of that land ends up sealed: yards, roads and parking around a single storey of storage ([[Land take of logistics buildings]]). Net land take in EU cities and their commuting zones reached 540 km² a year in 2018–2021 ([[Europe seals 1.5 km² of land every day]]). On the edge of a city, this land is often exactly where forests, fields and housing meet, so a logistics site cuts ecological corridors ([[Biodiversity and ecological connectivity on logistics sites]]), sends stormwater straight to drains ([[Stormwater and sealed surfaces on logistics sites]]) and presents a hard edge to its neighbours ([[Visual impact and acceptance by neighbours]]).

The question the studio asked is the one on Ece Yasar's poster: *what can logistics give back?*

## Projects

Seven student projects work with this approach, from a single roof to a whole district.

- [[Ece Yasar]] turns the roof of an existing cross-dock in Kitzingen into a planted bridge that crosses two roads at 7.0 m and reconnects two forest patches; the roof goes from 0% to 50% native planting, as reported by the student.
- [[Adam Večeřa]] stacks the IKEA store at Černý Most into a vertical tower and frees about 120,000 m² of ground for a park, a pond and an east–west corridor across the D10, as reported by the student.
- [[Natalie Žižková]] wraps a working distribution centre in Mississauga in a green hill and walkable roof terraces that link two separated parks.
- [[Lindsay Daphne Macuja]] reworks a big-box warehouse in Zličín into a last-mile hub with a green–blue buffer between two lakes.
- [[Marek Nový]] lays out a mixed industrial district in Otovice where tree belts, retention ponds and permeable surfaces are the infrastructure.
- [[Aycha Chambashala]] terraces an urban logistics centre in Bursa under green roofs, with bioswales and tree-canopy streets.
- [[Adéla Berkovitz]] splits a battery-recycling plant in Česká Lípa into three volumes with green roofs, an earth berm and a rainwater pond that doubles as fire-water reserve.

The exhibition also showed *Too Green To Be True!*, a contribution by landscape students of the Faculty of Horticulture of Mendel University in Brno (Lednice) (Markéta Bláhová, Martina Bobrovská, Helena Iwaniecová, Johana Joklová, Natálie Kruckovyčová). It catalogues the green tools of this strategy — vegetation, water, energy and building measures — on one idealised logistics park.

## Trade-offs

- **Green weight against carbon.** Green roofs and façades add structural weight and cost ([[Adam Večeřa]]); experts add that on a single-storey large-span roof this can massively increase the structure ([[Green roofs are a biodiversity investment, not a carbon one]], [[Green roofs vs LCA — an admitted conflict]]).
- **Shade against sun.** More trees and shaded public space lower direct solar exposure of the site from 92% to 77% ([[Marek Nový]]), with possible effects on daylight and PV.
- **Process against corridor.** A linear processing flow blocks a bio-corridor through the plot, so ecology moves to the perimeter; hazardous storage cannot carry a green roof ([[Adéla Berkovitz]]).
- **Compact massing against views.** Two large blocks worked operationally but scored low on residents' views and environmental performance ([[Aycha Chambashala]]).
- **Going vertical against digging deep.** Stacking frees ground but needs storage and parking 24 m below ground, against the project's own aim to avoid deep foundations ([[Adam Večeřa]]).

## Evidence gaps

- Most ecological values are targets or qualitative scores; calculation methods for green, biodiversity and water indicators are often not documented ([[Lindsay Daphne Macuja]], [[Marek Nový]], [[Aycha Chambashala]]).
- No project applies a recognised biodiversity net gain method end to end, as the Bestseller team did with BNG ([[BNG used outside Britain, as a design instrument]]).
- [[Ecological connectivity]] and [[Stormwater retention volume]] are used without any calculation tool, and tools that could compute [[Biodiversity net gain]] and [[Stormwater runoff reduction]] were not yet used in any project ([[Evidence gaps]]).
- The structural and carbon cost of green roofs is named as a trade-off but not calculated in any project.

## Regulatory observations

- Czech industrial sites must keep 20–30% of their area green; one developer argues this consumes about 30% more land and often produces greenery of little ecological value ([[Mandatory site greenery as anti-landscape policy]]). A requirement framed by ecological outcome rather than area share would reward the approach described here.
- Certification credits can be earned with token measures, such as a pile of stones as lizard habitat in the middle of a parking lot ([[Lizard habitat in a parking lot]]); where current standards count features rather than function, they limit the approach.
- On food-production sites, hygiene standards ask designers *not* to attract wildlife ([[Biodiversity forbidden by food-hygiene law]]), so the approach needs a different form there.
- Official carbon calculations do not accept planting as an offset, so benefits such as biodiversity and green corridors sit outside the carbon metric ([[Planting the felled forest back doesn't offset]]).
- Architects working for logistics developers see no market incentive to add greenery to logistics parks, unlike offices that compete for staff ([[Zero incentive for greenery in logistics]]); finance may move first, as banks with ESG policies can offer slightly better interest rates ([[The real green lever is 0.1–0.2 points of interest]]).

## Questions for practice

- **Developers:** Could a measured biodiversity gain be set as a target at concept stage, as on the Bestseller campus, instead of a share of green area?
- **Designers:** Where is the best place for nature on this site — on the roof, which adds structure, or on the ground, which ecologists consider more valuable?
- **Municipalities:** Could the mandatory green share be replaced by a requirement for connectivity to surrounding habitats and for keeping rainwater on site?
- **Everyone:** Who maintains the landscape after handover, and how is that written into the lease?
