## The question it answers

Does keeping the existing building save enough carbon to justify designing around it?

## How it works

1. Take the floor area of the existing building.
2. Multiply it by a benchmark embodied-carbon intensity for renovation and for demolition plus new construction.
3. Optionally add operational carbon and spread embodied carbon over an assumed lifespan, to compare per year.
4. Keep the building if the saving is large, and design the new programme around its structure.

## In the student projects

- [[Thomas Olsen]] — *manual / formula*. [[Embodied carbon saved by reuse]]: demolish + rebuild 1,566 tCO2e vs. renovation 450 tCO2e. Decision: “Keep and renovate the existing industrial structure instead of demolishing it, supported by the embodied carbon comparison (approx. 450 vs. 1,566 tCO2e).”
- [[Vladi Kostecki]] — *manual / formula*. [[Whole-life carbon]]: reconstruction 232.4 tCO2e/year vs. new build 745 tCO2e/year; [[Carbon per pallet handled]]: 1.45 kgCO2/pallet including embodied carbon. Decision: “Retain and reconstruct the existing industrial structure rather than build new, supported by the annualised carbon comparison (approx. 232 vs. 745 tCO2e/year).”
- [[Khrystyna Verbitska]] — *manual / formula*. [[Embodied carbon saved by reuse]]: approx. 210,000 kgCO2e saved by reuse, against 300,000 kgCO2e for new build. Decision: “Reuse an abandoned building on an inner-city brownfield instead of building new, which supports the embodied carbon saving and the green and public space gains.”

Values and decisions as reported by the student; see each case study for the page in the portfolio.

## Where it falls short

- All three projects use benchmark intensities rather than an LCA of the actual building, so the result depends on the benchmark chosen.
- In [[Vladi Kostecki]]'s project the derivation of the 745 tCO2e/year for new build is not shown.
