## Principle

Urban freight is treated as a designed network rather than as traffic that simply happens: goods are consolidated in small hubs close to demand and carried on by cargo bikes, light electric vehicles or drones. Where trucks remain, their yards and docks are laid out so tightly that land is freed for other uses, and the hub itself becomes a building the neighbourhood can live with.

## Why it matters

The last mile is the most visible and fragmented part of logistics: many small parcels, many stops, and constant pressure on streets and curbs ([[Last-mile delivery emissions and traffic]]). It is often quoted as 40–60% of total delivery cost, although this figure is disputed and the best-sourced value is about 41% ([[Last mile is shortest but costliest]]). Small depots of 5,000 m² or less are already the final building of the e-commerce chain ([[A two-to-three-tier building taxonomy]]), yet they usually arrive as fenced boxes or temporary containers ([[Urban integration of logistics buildings]]), with vans, bikes and pedestrians crossing paths ([[Pedestrian–truck safety on logistics sites]]). On larger hubs, the truck yard itself is the land problem ([[Truck flow and yard efficiency]], [[Land take of logistics buildings]]).

The studio asked Johannes Berger's question in a broader form: *what if there were fewer trucks — and what would the city get back?*

## Projects

Five student projects work with this approach, from one pavilion under a bridge to a city-wide network and a regional parcel hub.

- [[Eva Kažukolovaitė]] replaces the container micro-hub at Anděl Cycle Depot in Prague with modular timber units in a cross-dock layout that separates incoming vans from outgoing cargo bikes, moves the vehicle entrance away from pedestrians and adds a worker module, parcel lockers and a planted edge towards a new wetland park.
- [[Khrystyna Verbitska]] converts an abandoned concrete building in Háje into a micro-hub for cargo bikes and electric vans, stacked by floor: logistics on the ground, a showroom and repair level above, a public roof garden on top.
- [[Roshni Basu]] proposes a network of twelve neighbourhood micro-hubs for Prague, sited by population density, built from one modular "plug-in" unit that hides below ground in the historic centre and becomes a market-and-hub cluster in panelák estates.
- [[Johannes Berger]] asks what happens if trucks are replaced by drones, in three phases ending with a compact, vertical drone cross-dock of two towers linked by an elevated deck.
- [[Hynek Hrabík]] redesigns the truck yard of a PPL parcel hub as a flow-through system: trucks enter a corridor, reverse briefly into a bay and leave forward; a simulation shows 40 such docks match 60 conventional ones, as reported by the student.

For comparison, experts report that urban consolidation centres can cut freight vehicle trips by 20–50% in some cases, depending on location and use ([[Consolidation centres cut trips 20–50%]]), and that a redesigned compact electric cargo scooter gave a 30% efficiency gain over vans ([[Rethinking the vehicle beats electrifying it]]).

## Trade-offs

- **Clean vehicles against capacity.** Cargo bikes lower emissions and traffic but reduce throughput at peak demand ([[Khrystyna Verbitska]]).
- **Public programme against operations.** Mixing public uses with dispatch creates congestion and noise at peaks, so the developed scheme separates them by floor ([[Khrystyna Verbitska]]); public functions at a hub may also bring noise and foot traffic to residents ([[Eva Kažukolovaitė]]).
- **Hidden against cheap.** Putting hubs underground in the historic centre integrates them better but raises embodied carbon and cost; the panelák variant is cheaper and expects a higher solar share, 40–70% against 20–40% ([[Roshni Basu]]).
- **Height against carbon.** A vertical drone cross-dock saves land but raises embodied carbon, CapEx and operational energy; the drone network trades high upfront cost for lower running cost ([[Johannes Berger]]).
- **Fewer docks against tolerance.** Flow-through bays need conveyors and more automation, which use energy, and leave less room for imprecise manoeuvring ([[Hynek Hrabík]]).

## Evidence gaps

- Most delivery figures are literature values, not modelled for the project's own catchment; no project calculates vehicle-km or emissions for its own delivery model ([[Khrystyna Verbitska]], [[Johannes Berger]], [[Eva Kažukolovaitė]]).
- [[Last-mile delivery emissions]], [[Delivery time]], [[Delivery catchment]], [[Truck throughput]] and [[Pedestrian–vehicle conflict points]] are used without any calculation tool, and no tool in the knowledge base yet covers them ([[Evidence gaps]]).
- KPI charts are shown without numbers: unlabelled before/after bars in [[Roshni Basu]], and environmental and turnaround indicators in [[Hynek Hrabík]].
- Hub locations are chosen from density maps or site availability, not from freight-flow data, which experts name as the general weakness of hub siting ([[Hub placement decided without data]]).
- The flow-through simulation is the group's only operational model; traffic-engineering review is pending ([[Hynek Hrabík]]).

## Regulatory observations

- Urban vehicle access regulations, which set which vehicles may enter which areas and when, are described as a strong policy that already pushes operators towards smaller electric vehicles and time windows ([[Access regulation is the strongest design lever]]).
- New vehicle types can run ahead of their legal category: a compact four-wheel electric cargo scooter was not yet regulated in every country of its pilot ([[Innovation blocked by missing regulation, not missing technology]]). Where current standards have no category, they limit the approach.
- Delivery robots have no defined place in the street section, and dedicated robot lanes do not yet exist in Europe ([[Delivery robots have no legal place in the street section]]).
- Hubs need land inside or at the edge of the city; where zoning plans do not provide it, logistics tends to take space opportunistically ([[If cities don't plan for logistics, logistics takes space]]).
- Loading zones are widely misused and still enforced by manual patrols ([[Half of loading zones misused]]); time-based curb use with digital booking would support the small-hub model.

## Questions for practice

- **Developers:** Could a city-edge parcel hub be planned around a measured number of docks and truck throughput, as in the flow-through simulation, so that the saved apron becomes green or leasable land?
- **Designers:** How can a micro-hub share its building with the public — by floor, by edge, or by time of day — without conflict between people and vehicles?
- **Municipalities:** Could hub locations be chosen with freight-flow data and secured in zoning plans, instead of left to whatever space becomes available?
- **Everyone:** Which vehicle — cargo bike, light electric vehicle or drone — fits which street, and who decides where it may operate?
