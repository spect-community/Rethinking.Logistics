## The problem

Certificates and norms can be met without fulfilling their intention. Design teams focus on achieving BREEAM credits rather than on the building as a whole, and the scheme tends to dictate design solutions instead of focusing on the intention behind them ([[Schweber & Haroglu (2014) – The impact of BREEAM on the design of buildings]]). Most rating schemes do not set biodiversity objectives at all ([[Evaluating biodiversity criteria in green building rating schemes]]).

The challenge notes carry a *Common misreading* section that shows the difference — see [[Habitat loss and cut ecological corridors]] and [[Designing for an unknown tenant]].

## What the evidence says

- [[Design teams chase BREEAM credits, not the building]] — Design teams focus on achieving BREEAM credits rather than on the building as a whole.
- [[85% of rating schemes set no biodiversity objectives]] — Most green building rating schemes do not specify biodiversity objectives (85 %).
- [[BREEAM Outstanding added 4.82% to capital cost]] — The capital cost uplift for BREEAM Outstanding on a large UK warehouse was +4.82 %, and under 0.5 % for Very Good or Excellent.
- [[Sustainability arrives as a minimum, not an ambition]] — Developers install only the minimum EV charging needed to satisfy a BREEAM-type standard; more only if the tenant explicitly asks. He reports never being asked about electric or hydrogen trucks at all in CEE.
- [[A greenwash premium of about 10%]] — There are companies charging roughly 10% more for materials branded "green something or other" that are neither clever nor particularly low carbon.
- [[Measure it or you're just claiming it]] — Commitment to quantifiable nature-positive outcomes is what separates real ecological performance from a green facade as marketing.
- [[Certificates are a language for banks]] — Certification's real function is as a common language linking investors, tenants and banks. Banks tie loan volume to rating level: gold unlocks the full sum, silver a smaller one.
