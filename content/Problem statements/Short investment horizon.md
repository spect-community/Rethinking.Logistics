## The problem

Logistics warehouses are mostly developed speculatively, as short-term investment products: the first owner is usually a developer-trader who sells at or before completion, so long-term operating benefits are not captured in the developer's return. At the design stage nobody knows who the tenant will be, so the building is sized for any possible tenant — see [[Designing for an unknown tenant]].

## What the evidence says

- [[Logistics warehouses are built for a five-year exit]] — Logistics warehouses are typically developed as short-term investment products, with an exit or payback horizon of about five years.
- [[Developer margins of 3–5%]] — Developer margins on logistics projects are around 3–5 %.
- [[Long-hold warehouses cost 10–15% more to build]] — Long-hold or owner-occupier warehouses cost about 10–15 % more to build.
- [[Unknown tenants make speculative warehouses oversized]] — Speculative warehouses are oversized because the tenant, its needs and the lease length are unknown at design stage.
- [[A 160 bps development spread for big-box logistics]] — The development spread for European big-box logistics was about 160 basis points in January 2025.
- [[Lease length is the hidden design brief]] — Logistics tenants sign 5–7 year leases; manufacturers sign 15, and increasingly 20–25 years, because relocation costs are prohibitive.
- [[80% of the market need, not 100% of one tenant]] — The investor horizon is 15–20 years while leases run 5–10; a building must fit roughly 80% of market demand so tenants can be swapped without touching the asset's fundamentals.
- [[Tailoring is a one-way door]] — Buildings can be tailor-made "to the point where it's not usable for the future", and with Amazon or H&M investing multiples of the building's value in technology, the developer has no choice. Since the chance that another tenant uses such a building the same way after ~20 years is very limited, it must be possible to recycle it easily, ideally on site.
